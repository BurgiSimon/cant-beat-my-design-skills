Field Notes/Launch stories

Sixty pots in three hours

Inés Navarro had never run a sale, kept a spreadsheet or written a launch plan. Her autumn drop of sixty hand-thrown pieces sold out before lunch. She wrote down how, mostly so she can remember.

IN

Inés NavarroPotter and first-time founder, Valencia

15 Oct 2026·4 min read·Filed in Make

My kiln holds sixty pieces if I pack it carefully and say something kind to it. So a drop, for Kiln & Co., is sixty pieces. That is the whole business plan, and for two years it was also the whole of my planning.

Until this autumn I sold pots the way most potters do: a few at the market, a few to friends, a post whenever I remembered. Then my sister Lucía, who has opinions and a marketing job, told me to do a proper drop. One date, one page, everything at once. I said yes before I understood what that meant.

I am not a project manager

The first thing I did was open Crestora and panic slightly at the word “lane”. The second was to discover I didn’t need to understand it. I picked a Blueprint template written by another potter, and it asked me five questions: what are you making, how many, when does it go on sale, who’s helping, and what does done look like? Done, I wrote, looks like an empty shelf.

It turned my answers into milestones on my calendar. Throwing, trimming, bisque firing, glazing, glaze firing, photographs, listings, sale. Eight things. I can manage eight things.

I’m not a project manager. Crestora never asked me to become one. It kept the list, so I could keep my hands in the clay.

The making is the long part

Fourteen days of making: forty-two mugs, twelve bowls and six vases that wanted to be bowls. Every evening I photographed the shelf and dropped the picture on the lane from my phone, with clay on the screen. It sounds like nothing. By the second week, the lane was a diary. I could scroll back and watch the mugs drying, the first bisque coming out, and the glaze test from kiln two that came out the colour of a bruise.

The kiln sets the pace, not me. A bisque firing takes a day and a night, the glaze firing another, and nothing can be hurried while it cools. Because each firing was a milestone on the lane, the photographs, the prices and the shop listing slotted into the waiting time instead of piling up at the end.

Seven pieces cracked in the glaze firing. That is normal, and it still hurts. I threw seven more on the Sunday, and they made it with a day to spare.

Thirty-one notes

Lucía reviewed everything: photos, prices, the words under each pot. I sent her a Review Room link, and she pinned her notes straight onto the pictures. “This one looks bigger than it is, put a hand next to it.” “Nobody knows what speckled stoneware means. Say it looks like a quail’s egg.” She left thirty-one notes. I argued with nine of them and lost seven. The two I won were both about the vases, which I still think are perfect.

Saturday, ten o’clock

The sale opened on Saturday 10 October at 10:00. Countdown fired everything at the same minute: Postbox sent the newsletter, Shopfront opened the shop, and the posts went out while I sat at the kitchen table not touching anything. Lucía came round with pastries. We watched the launch room together: a counter of pieces left, and a little line of orders arriving from Valencia, Bilbao, Lyon and, for reasons nobody can explain, a lot of Rotterdam.

The first mug sold in forty seconds. The last vase sold at 13:04. Sixty pieces in three hours and four minutes. I cried a bit, then packed boxes until midnight.

What changes in spring

Afterglow showed me things I would never have counted. Half the orders came from people who opened the newsletter in its first ten minutes, so the list matters more than the posts. The mugs were gone in forty minutes and the vases took three hours, so the spring drop has more mugs and fewer vases. And eleven people wrote to ask whether I would ever do a second firing. That one went back to the Sparkboard.

The spring drop is in Review now. Lucía has left four notes. Four. I’m choosing to believe that means I’ve learned something, and not that she’s busy.
