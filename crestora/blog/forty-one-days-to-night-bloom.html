Field Notes/Launch stories

Forty-one days to Night Bloom

A seven-minute animated short about a garden that only opens after dark, made by three people on two continents. Amara Okafor on the thread she used to lose, and the night she didn’t.

AO

Amara OkaforAnimator, Lagos

12 Nov 2026·4 min read·Filed in Afterglow

The idea arrived as a voice memo. Saturday 19 September, a little after one in the morning, recorded on the walk home from my cousin’s wedding in Surulere: “A garden that only opens after dark. Everyone on the street knows about it. Nobody admits they go.” Forty-one days later, two hundred people sat on a rooftop in Yaba and watched it open.

I have made short films before. What I had never done is finish one on the day I said I would. This is how Night Bloom got there.

Sparks first, sense later

For the first three days I didn’t plan anything. I threw everything at the Sparkboard: the memo, photos of my aunt’s night jasmine, a palette lifted from an old cinema poster, a gate drawn on the back of a receipt. Crestora grouped them into constellations on its own, and when I opened the board on the Monday, the film was already sitting there in three clusters: the street, the gate, the garden. I promoted the garden to a lane, and that was that.

One page, three people

The brief took an afternoon. One page in Blueprints: what it is (seven minutes, no dialogue, a single night), who is riding along, and what done looks like: a rooftop screening on Friday 30 October, because the roof was free that night and nowhere else was.

Chidi, who paints backgrounds better than anyone I know, lives twenty minutes away. Lena, who does sound, lives in Kraków. I sent them both the same link. Nobody had to make an account to read it, and nobody asked me “what’s the deadline again?” for the rest of the project, which is a first.

The thread I used to lose

On every film before this one, the thing that broke me was the gap between storyboard and render. The storyboard lived in one app, the animatic in another, the renders in a shared folder with names like final_v7_REAL.mov, and the notes lived in group chats, where they went to die. By the time a note reached me, I couldn’t tell which version it was about.

On Night Bloom, the storyboard, the animatic and every render sat on the same lane, one after another. When I dropped in render seven, Workbench showed me exactly which shots had changed since six, and Chidi’s notes on the backgrounds were still pinned to the frames they were about. Twenty-two days of making, and I never once opened a folder to look for something.

Two hundred and fourteen frames

Review was the part I was dreading. Kraków and Lagos keep almost the same clock, but Lena mixes at dawn, before her day job, and I animate until four in the morning, so we were never awake at the same time. We stopped trying. She watched each new cut in Review Room with her first coffee and pinned notes straight onto frames, with the exact second of sound attached: “the crickets drop out a beat too early here.” I woke up to them in order, fixed them in order and sent them back before I slept.

Chidi reviewed the backgrounds the same way, from his flat, at a sensible hour, which made him the only well-rested person on the project. We reviewed 214 frames in eight days. Frame 118, where the gate first opens, took three rounds. When Lena finally pressed Approve, the lane rolled on to Launch by itself, and I went and made a very large cup of tea.

A premiere on a roof

Countdown built the launch checklist from the brief, so the dull things got done: the festival upload, the captions, the press kit, the post that went out to every channel at 21:00. I didn’t have to remember any of it, which meant I could worry about the projector instead.

On the night, the crew watched the launch room from two continents: Chidi on the roof with me, Lena on her sofa with a slice of cake she claimed was for the film. At 21:00 the counter reached zero, the chip turned to Live, and two hundred people on a rooftop went quiet for seven minutes.

What the afterglow said

A week later, Afterglow sent the day 7 recap: 12.4 thousand views, 318 comments, 2.1 thousand saves. The number I keep coming back to is smaller. One comment, from someone I have never met: “Watched it twice on the bus home.” It’s pinned above my desk.

Afterglow also sent the ideas back. Three comments asked what happens to the girl who keeps the gate. She is on my Sparkboard now, in a constellation of her own.

And the film has one more road to ride. A festival cut, with a new score by Tunde Bello, has its own lane now, and on Friday 11 December it launches online, for everyone who couldn’t fit on the roof.
