Designing Review Room

Feedback that doesn’t stall the engine. Hye-jin Park, who leads design for Review Room, on the three ideas it was built on, the one we got wrong, and why it learned to listen this week.

HP

Hye-jin ParkDesign lead, Review Room, Seoul

20 Nov 2026·4 min read·Filed in Review

Every creative project has a moment where it stops. The work is done, or nearly, and it goes out for feedback, and then nothing happens for nine days. Somebody is waiting on somebody. The file was the wrong version. The note said “the bit near the end”, and there are three bits near the end.

When we started designing Review Room in 2023, we spent the first month not designing anything. We sat with eleven creators while they asked for feedback and got it, and we wrote down every time the work stopped moving. We counted forty-three stalls that month. Nearly all of them came down to one of three things.

A note belongs to a moment

The first was ambiguity. “Can the logo be bigger?” is a fine note on a poster and a useless one on a ninety-second film with the logo in eleven places. So the first rule of Review Room was that every note is pinned to something exact: a frame, a pixel, a second of audio, a line of code. You can’t leave a note in Review Room that isn’t somewhere.

That one rule shaped everything else. Notes in a margin feel like a conversation; notes on the work feel like a list. We wanted the list. The maker opens a version and sees its notes in order, each one where it belongs, and ticks them off one by one. When a new version arrives, the open notes travel with it: a note on line 40 of a script follows its line to 52, and a note on frame 118 stays on frame 118 even after the edit shortens the cut.

Reviewers aren’t users

The second stall was access. The people whose opinions count most are the least likely to sign up for anything: the client, the label, the director’s mother. In the first version of Review Room, a reviewer could open a link and watch, but had to sign in to leave a note. We told ourselves it was about knowing who said what.

It was a mistake, and the numbers told us so for two years before we listened. A third of review links were opened and never commented on. In August we removed the sign-in. Reviewers now type their name and leave notes; the link can expire, ask for a password or watermark the work, and every note is still on the record. Comments on review links went up 58 % in the first month.

Approval is a button

The third stall came at the end. The work would be finished, everyone would agree it was finished, and it would still sit there because nobody had said so in writing. So Review Room has an Approve button, and when the last reviewer presses it, the lane moves on to Launch by itself. Approvals record who, when and which version; on Crew plans they keep a full audit trail, because some clients need one.

It is the smallest feature in Review Room and the one creators thank us for most. Today, 71 % of reviews in Crestora close within a day.

Learning to listen

This week’s release, v4.12, adds what podcasters and musicians have asked for since the beginning. Review Room now draws audio as a waveform, and you can pin a note to a moment of sound: click anywhere on the wave, say what you heard, and the note sits at that exact second, with half a second either side so the maker hears it in context.

It was harder than it sounds. Video has frames you can look at; sound only exists while it plays. Our first prototype paused playback for every note, and the forty podcasters testing it hated it: you lose the feel of a mix every time it stops. The version that shipped keeps playing while you type, and pins the note to where you clicked, not to wherever the playhead has reached by the time you press Enter. Open notes sit on the wave in amber; when the maker resolves one, it turns green, so a glance at the waveform tells you how much of the mix is settled.

What we’re still working on

Notes on 3D files are next on the roadmap. So is something quieter: we want Review Room to notice when a review has stalled and nudge the right person, once, kindly. If you’ve had a review get stuck and you can tell us why, I would love to hear about it at hello@crestora.example.
