The case for a smaller launch

We build the tool that launches your work to fourteen channels at once. Rafael Mendes, who works on Countdown, makes the case for launching it to forty people instead.

RM

Rafael MendesEngineer, Countdown, Lisbon

6 Nov 2026·4 min read·Filed in Shape

I work on Countdown, the part of Crestora that schedules a launch to fourteen channels at once and opens a room where your crew watches it go live. So it may sound strange coming from me: most launches are too big.

Not too ambitious. Too big. Too many channels, too many people, too much riding on one night. Big launches feel brave, but in my experience they are often a way of not deciding who the thing is actually for.

Forty seats on a closed street

The best launch I have seen this year had forty people at it. Tomás Ruiz runs Long Table, a supper club in Lisbon. In September he decided to close a street in Graça for one night and seat forty strangers at a single table down the middle of it.

He opened a lane on a Tuesday. By Friday it had a Blueprint, a menu, a street permit, a playlist and exactly one launch channel: an email to the 212 people who had eaten with him before. Forty seats went in an hour. Nine days after the first note, forty strangers sat down under strings of lights and passed the bread.

He didn’t post it anywhere else. He didn’t need to. Everyone who wanted to be there heard about it from the one place they already trusted him.

The rest of the lane was just as small. On the Monday he cooked the whole menu for six friends and let them pin notes to it: less salt in the clams, the dessert too sweet after that much wine, a jumper for anyone who forgot that Lisbon gets cold after ten. By the morning after the dinner he had thirty-one replies, two of them asking to help with the next one. The next one is already on his Sparkboard.

Small is a decision

A smaller launch is not a launch with less effort. It is a launch with fewer things left to chance. When you choose one channel, you can make that channel good. When you choose forty people, you can know who they are. When you choose one night, you can be there for all of it.

Countdown is happy to schedule one channel. The checklist gets shorter, the launch room gets quieter, and you can read every reply that comes back. That is not a lesser launch. It is one you can actually pay attention to.

Here are the four questions I ask before I schedule anything in Countdown, my own work included:

  1. Who are the first forty people this is for? Can I name them?
  2. Where do they already listen? That’s the channel. One, maybe two.
  3. What do I want them to do in the first hour? Make that the only button.
  4. What will I do with what they tell me? If the answer is “nothing”, it’s too early to launch.

If you can’t answer the first one, a bigger launch won’t help. It will only tell more people that you weren’t sure.

Three hundred copies

I am taking my own advice. Outside work I make records, and on Friday 4 December a 7‑inch of fado covers comes out: two songs, pressed in Porto, three hundred copies. It will not go to fourteen channels. It goes to one: a letter to the people who came to the four gigs I played this year, with a link and a date. If they buy a hundred, I’ll be thrilled. If they buy three hundred, I will press more and tell a few more people.

Small launches compound

Here is the part that surprised me. Looking across thousands of lanes in Afterglow, creators whose first launch went to a single channel launched their second thing much sooner: a median of 19 days later, against 47 days for first launches sent to five channels or more. A small launch is survivable. You learn, you fix, you go again. A huge launch that lands softly can take a year to get over.

So use all fourteen channels when the work is ready for the whole world. But the next time you’re about to launch something you made, ask whether it would be better as a dinner for forty. It usually is.
